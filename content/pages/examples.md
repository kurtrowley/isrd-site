# What it looks like in use

Four short walk-throughs. Each is an **illustration**: an imagined case built from how the tools work, and real cases will follow. Each says which parts exist today and which are planned.

## A. The clinic waiting list

*Tools used: Models, Writing Studio.*

**The situation.** You are a systems designer called in by a clinic manager. Waiting times keep growing. Each year the clinic adds appointments, and each year the list is longer again.

**What you do**

1. In the Studio, start a project and describe the problem in a note. *(Live)*
2. Ask which things pile up, which drain them, and what feeds back. Draw the picture: the waiting list is a pile; referrals flow in; appointments flow out; but more appointments make the clinic known, and referrals rise. *(Live, as a map; a tutor that asks the questions is planned.)*
3. You see that added appointments pull in more referrals. You mark this as a **likely parallel**, a pattern seen in other services, and note what you and the manager would need to check in the clinic's figures.
4. Run a simple model with the clinic's numbers and try three options: more appointments, a triage step, a follow-up call. *(Planned)*
5. Write a one-page brief for the clinic's board. The AI may offer a sketch of a paragraph; you test it and put it in your own words. *(Live)*

**What you leave with.** A picture of the loop, a short list of options with what each is likely to do and how sure we are, and a brief the clinic's board can read.

**Who did what.** The tool asks questions, keeps the pieces together and runs the numbers. You decide what is true of the clinic and what to recommend, with the manager and staff.

## B. A reporter and a budget

*Tools used: Writing Studio, Models.*

**The situation.** A city budget is up for a vote, and you have a week to explain what it will really change.

**What you do.** Collect sources and notes in one place *(live)*. Map who pays, who gains and what changes if a number moves *(live)*. Test your claims against your sources with the reviewer *(live)*. Draft your story yourself, using sketches only where you ask for them *(live)*. Run a simple model of what the budget does over five years *(planned)*.

**What you leave with.** A story whose claims you can trace to sources, and a picture readers can follow.

## C. A project leader and a local issue

*Tools used: Model builder, Writing Studio, Library.*

**The situation.** You lead a group trying to reduce street flooding. People are willing to help and are looking for a way in.

**What you do.** Build a simple model of the local system with the people who live in it *(planned)*. Each person works out what they can do *(a guided note, live; a tutor to help, planned)*. Write up the plan, a short story for neighbours, and a proposal for the council *(live)*. Put the model and the plan on a page of your own, linked to other projects *(planned)*.

**What you leave with.** A shared picture, individual roles, and the writing to move the effort forward.

## D. A team that has to learn a new skill

*Tools used: Practice games, Writing Studio.*

**The situation.** Your team must learn to write proposals that win funding, or to review safety reports, or whatever the expert skill is.

**What you do.** Set the studio up for that work from a short set of questions *(planned; today the setup is for writing)*. Practise on a small invented case in a safe game, then do the real piece, with the same steps *(live for writing; planned for other skills)*.

**What you leave with.** People who have practised the real process, and a record of how each did.

## About these examples

Each moves between tools: A starts in a model and finishes in the Studio; B starts in the Studio and checks a claim in a model; C builds a model and writes it up; D practises in a game and then does the real piece. When real people agree to be case studies, they will replace these.
