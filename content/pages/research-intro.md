We do **applied research and design**. We take what the systems sciences, and the sciences of each field, already know. We apply it to real problems. We build tools to make that knowledge usable, and we find out whether the tools do any good.

We build on the theory of the systems sciences and put it to work.

Our question is practical: **when people use tools like these, what changes?**

### The questions

1. Do they understand the system better (and can we tell)?
2. Do they choose better, or just more confidently?
3. Do they act, and does the action fit what they learned?
4. Do they stay in charge of their own judgment, or hand it to the tool?
5. Does the tool work for newcomers as well as experts, and for people in different fields?
6. What side effects do the tools have, such as time, cost, false confidence, or who gets left out?

### How we find out

- **Design experiments.** We build a version, give it to a small group of people who do the work, watch and listen, collect their feedback as they go, change it, and do it again until it works. This is slow, and it tells us a lot about why things work or fail.
- **Use over time, with consent.** How people use the Studio and where they stop. We keep personal data separate from the research and share nothing that identifies you.
- **Checks on models.** A field expert reviews any model that carries a project's name, and we publish the assumptions.
- **Comparisons, where we can arrange them fairly.** Two similar groups, one using the tool, compared on a task where the result can be judged.

We use small groups for most of this. Small groups give trustworthy design insight, and we say how many people took part and who they were.

### What we measure

These are proposed measures, each something we can observe:

- Could the person draw and explain the loop in their own situation afterwards, in a way a domain expert agrees with?
- Did they leave with something usable: a brief, a plan, a model, a piece of writing?
- Did the options they weighed change, and did they say why?
- How long did it take, and what did it cost?
- Did they follow up and use it, a month later?
- Whose judgment was in the result? (We keep the sketches apart, so we can tell.)
- What confused people, and what did we change?

### Where this comes from

The approach has a long lineage in the founder's work. A 1995 doctoral study at Florida State University built an electronic tool for designing education systems and tested it over four rounds with educators. The educators found it useful, wanted a simple start, preferred plain language to a factory metaphor, and asked for shared parts that people could borrow and contribute to. The present work carries that idea, a connected system of tools with a shared library, beyond education to systemic change in any field. Later work includes a study of a computer tutor for writers (Rowley and Meyer, 2003) and the long design experiment behind the Writing Studio, described in [Designing Toward a Theory](/research/papers/designing-toward-a-theory/). See also the [research methodology and publications](/research/methodology-and-publications/).

### Our commitments

We will publish what we find, whatever it shows. We will say how many people took part, who they were, and how we measured. We will label each claim with how sure we are, and we will claim only what we have measured.
