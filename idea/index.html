The idea

The idea

Tools that organize, simulate and train, with you in charge.

Complex work is hard for a reason

A hospital, a newsroom, a city budget, a climate policy: each has too many connected parts to hold in one head. We cope by keeping the parts in separate boxes. Then something moves in one box and surprises us in another.

We think good tools do three jobs.

  • They organize. They keep the pieces and the connections where you can see them.
  • They simulate. They show what a choice is likely to produce, before you make it.
  • They train. They help you get better at choosing, rather than choosing for you.

The third job matters most. A tool that decides for you leaves you weaker. We build tools where you stay in charge.

Why a tool and not just an AI chat

You can ask a chatbot almost anything. A chat does not know your whole project. It forgets what you decided last week. It will not run the same model twice and show you the same result. It does not tell you where an idea came from.

A good tool follows the steps an expert takes, keeps the whole project in one place, runs real models you can repeat, notes where things came from, and checks the work. The AI helps. You decide.

Two ways we use AI, kept apart

When an AI drafts words, we call them a sketch. It is labelled, set apart from your writing, and meant to be tested and rewritten. When we run a model, we mean a runnable picture of a situation, with its assumptions in plain sight.

Tools that connect people, too

Hard changes are made by many people, not one. So the tools are designed to connect. What one person makes (a model, a map, a plan, a piece of research) can be picked up, tested and built on by someone else, and each person sees it in the way their role needs. A public library of shared parts, and a community of people who use and improve them, are part of the design. They are planned, and we are working out the details. See Tools and Community.

How sure are we?

  • A known pattern: well tested in many places.
  • A likely parallel: it looks the same in a new place, and we think it carries over.
  • A guess worth testing: an idea we are curious about and cannot yet support.

Hard things get built in loops

Say you are planning a garden. You sketch the whole plot. Then you dig one bed and plant it. Now you see that the fence throws shade across half the plot, so the tomatoes must move, so the path must move, so the whole plan changes. You redraw the plot, then dig another bed.

Nobody plans a garden all at once and then carries out the plan. The plan and the beds teach each other.

This is how complicated things get made. A small part is built and tested against the whole, and the whole changes in response. Then another part. It has many names: in nature it is variation and selection, over many generations; in engineering it is a short sprint, then a look at the whole system; in research it is a design experiment, where you build, test, learn and rebuild. The shape is the same, and in each field the details differ.

How sure are we? A likely parallel. The shape matches across these fields, and we think it carries over. We do not say they are the same thing. Evolution does not choose, and you do.

Writing is a loop, too

People are taught that writing goes plan, draft, revise. Expert writers don't work that way. They sketch the whole (who it is for, what it claims, how it hangs together), build out one section as a try, and then look at what it did to the whole. The claim may need to be sharper. The order may change. A promise made in the opening may be broken. So they reshape the whole, and build the next part.

The finished piece wasn't planned at the start. It came out of many turns of this loop. Writers' own methods, such as starting with a sentence and growing it outward, are ways of working the same loop on purpose.

What the Writing Studio does about it

The Writing Studio is built around the loop. We are building it to help you:

  • keep the whole in sight (purpose, reader, claim, plan) next to the part you are working on;
  • move between whole, section and passage, and come back;
  • try a part as a prototype before you commit to it;
  • ask "what did this change?" after you build something, and check the claim, the order and your promises;
  • see the history of the whole, so you can tell how the piece changed its mind;
  • bring in the craft of your kind of writing, in the right place;
  • build a model of what the piece is about, and see what a reader needs to know before a given point;
  • practise the loop on a small case, so it becomes how you work.

What is live and what is planned. Today the Studio holds the pieces: notes, sources, maps, versions and a reviewer. The features that make the loop itself explicit (the strategy card, trying a part, "what did this change?", the history of the whole, the practice) are planned. We will say here when each arrives.

The same loop in every tool

Whichever tool you pick up, the same thing happens: build a little, look at the whole, change the whole.

For everyday work

Nothing here is only for specialists. Anyone who handles something with many connected parts, such as a household budget, a school timetable or a team project, works this way when it goes well. If the idea catches on, we hope this way of working becomes ordinary in all kinds of daily work. That is a hope, and we are testing it: we do not know yet.
